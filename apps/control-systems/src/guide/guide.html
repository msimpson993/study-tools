Q1

Why model at all?

A controller is a bet on how the plant will react. A model is what makes that bet informed: it predicts the output for any input, so you can design and test on paper or in Simulink before touching hardware.

Every design question in this course (“How much gain?”, “Will it overshoot?”, “Will it settle on target?”, “What happens when a load hits?”) is answered by running the model, not the machine. The model doesn't need to be perfect. Feedback (Stage 13) exists partly because models are never perfect. But it has to capture the dominant behavior.

Why it mattersEvery later topic (poles, ζ, steady-state error, sensitivity) is a property of a model. If the model is wrong in the dominant dynamics, every number you compute from it is wrong too.
Q2

Why Laplace transforms and transfer functions?

For linear time-invariant (LTI) systems, Laplace turns differential equations into algebra: d/dt becomes multiplication by s, and convolution becomes multiplication. Systems in series then simply multiply.

Physical laws (KVL, Newton) give ODEs. Solving ODEs for every input is painful, and in the time domain chaining two systems means a convolution integral (Lecture 1: y(t) = ∫ x(τ)h(t−τ)dτ). In the s-domain the same chain is Y = H·X. That's why block diagrams work: each block is a multiplier.

The price: linear (superposition holds: no saturation, no dead zones) and time-invariant (constant coefficients: R, L, C, m don't drift). Real plants are approximately LTI around an operating point, and that approximation is good enough for most of the design.

You already think this way with impedances: Z = jωL is just sL evaluated at s = jω. Transfer functions are the same move, with s allowed off the jω axis to capture transients.

Recognize
  • s ↔ d/dt, 1/s ↔ ∫dt (with zero initial conditions).
  • “Blocks in series multiply” only holds in the s-domain. In time it's convolution.
  • Saturation, dead zones, or time-varying parameters mean you've left the LTI world, and transfer function results are approximations.
Q3

What does “zero initial conditions” mean, and what doesn't it mean?

A transfer function is defined with every initial condition set to zero, so it describes how the system responds to an input starting from rest. It doesn't claim real systems start at rest. It just leaves the initial-condition response out.

Laplace of a derivative is sX(s) − x(0). Setting x(0) = 0 for every stored quantity (capacitor voltage, inductor current, position, velocity) removes those extra terms, and the output becomes a pure ratio times the input: Y(s) = G(s)U(s).

Trap
  • A transfer function can't show you the response to a charged capacitor with zero input. You need the ODE or state space (which carries x(0) naturally) for that.
  • Because the system is linear, the full response = (zero-state response from G) + (zero-input response from the initial conditions). The transfer function only gives the first part.
Q4

Poles and zeros: what are they telling me?

Poles (roots of the denominator) are the system's natural modes: each pole p puts a term ept in the response. Zeros (roots of the numerator) are input shapes the system blocks, like transmission notches. Poles decide stability and speed; zeros change how the modes are weighted.

Recognize
  • Real part of the pole → decay rate (envelope eσt). Imaginary part → oscillation frequency.
  • Any pole with Re > 0 means the system is unstable, no matter what the others do.
  • Complex poles always come in conjugate pairs for physical (real-coefficient) systems.
  • A zero right on top of a pole cancels it in G(s), but the mode can still exist internally (see Q20).

S1

How are poles and zeros drawn, and why are they marked differently?

Poles are drawn as × and zeros as ○. Poles are where |G(s)| blows up to ∞; zeros are where G(s) = 0. Picture |G(s)| as a rubber sheet over the plane: poles are tent poles pushing it up to infinity, and zeros are tacks pinning it down to zero.
Recognize
  • × = pole = root of the denominator. ○ = zero = root of the numerator. Exam plots, MATLAB pzmap, and the Lecture Notes all use this convention.
  • The picture dictionary in Q4 shows only poles (×), because it's about natural modes. Zeros have no mode of their own.
  • Evaluating G on the jω axis (s = jω) is the frequency response: walking up the axis past a × gives a resonant bump, and past a ○ gives a notch.

Exactly like a filter's pole/zero plot: a high-Q pole pair close to the jω axis is a sharp resonance, and a zero on the axis is a perfect notch (a trap circuit).

Deep dive

What does a point on the s-plane actually mean? (the |G| surface picture)

If the s-plane feels slippery, it's usually because three very different things are being pictured at the same spot: the system, the input you apply, and the knobs you turn. One picture keeps them apart. We'll use it for this whole deep dive.

⛰️
The picture: |G| as a 3D surface over the s-plane Lay the s-plane flat and, above every point s, raise a surface to height |G(s)|. That's the whole transfer function drawn as one landscape (the phase ∠G(s) is a second number attached to each point). Poles × are peaks (the surface shoots to ∞), and zeros ○ are sinkholes (it touches 0). A Bode magnitude plot is just a cut through this surface along the jω axis (σ = 0): slice the landscape with a vertical plane standing on the jω axis, and the edge you see is |G(jω)|. Left, on, and right of that axis describe natural modes, not geography: a pole in the LHP is a mode that decays, one on the axis rings forever, and one in the RHP grows, even with no input (M8).

Three misconceptions this kills
  • “A control setting is a position on the plane.” No. A knob is a bulldozer: it reshapes the surface (moves the ×'s). It doesn't put you anywhere.
  • “A point is the drive signal” or “a point is the response.” No. A point is an address where you evaluate G. The input is a time signal (sin ωt). The output is that same sine scaled by the surface height and shifted by the phase.
  • “Any point can be a sine drive.” No. A steady sine of frequency ω lives only on the jω axis, at s = ±jω. Points off the axis correspond to sinusoids that grow or decay exponentially.

M6

If the ◆ is one probe signal, what is my real input?

Your real input is a combination, literally a sum, of many probe signals est, each with its own weight. That's what a transform does: Fourier writes a signal as a sum of probes on the jω axis (pure sines, s = jω). Laplace allows probes anywhere (s = σ + jω, growing or decaying). Linearity lets you handle each probe separately: response/probe = G(s) at that probe's address. Then add up all the responses.

M7

Does the distance from the ◆ to a pole or zero matter?

Yes. It's the whole geometric meaning of G(s). Write the explorer's system in factored form, G(s) = C·(s − z)/((s − p)(s − p̄)). Each factor (s − mark) is a complex number, and you can draw it as an arrow: the vector from that mark to the ◆. Those are exactly the dashed lines in the explorer. Read them like this:
  • Length of a dashed line = |s − mark| = how much that mark contributes to |G|.
  • Angle of a dashed line (measured from the +σ direction) = that mark's contribution to ∠G.
📏
The rule (magnitudes multiply, angles add)

|G(s)| = |C| · (product of distances ◆ → each zero ○)(product of distances ◆ → each pole ×)

∠G(s) = ∠C + Σ(angles from zeros) − Σ(angles from poles)

Why this matters
  • Resonance, explained by a ruler. Slide the ◆ up the jω axis past a lightly damped pole (try the “light damping” preset). At the ω just beside the ×, one dashed line becomes very short, so the denominator shrinks and |G| spikes. That's the Bode peak. The closer the pole is to the jω axis (smaller ζ), the shorter that shortest line can get, and the taller the peak.
  • Notches, same idea upside down. A zero near the jω axis makes a numerator line short at that ω, so |G| dips.
  • Phase, too. As the ◆ walks past a pole, that pole's arrow swings through about 180°, which is why the phase drops quickly near resonance.
RecognizeCheck it live: the readout lists each distance and the product |C|·d○/(d×·d×̄). It matches |G(s)| every time. Drag the ◆ next to a × and watch that dashed line shrink while |G| climbs.
TrapTwo different distances get mixed up. Distance from the origin to a pole is ωn = |p|, a property of the system (how fast that mode is). Distance from the ◆ to a pole is lookup geometry: it changes every time you move the probe and tells you how big |G| is at that address. Same ruler, different question.
M8

Do RHP poles mean the system blows up even with no input?

Yes. No input is required. A pole is an est the system can produce on its own (that's why |G| → ∞ there: zero input, nonzero output). A pole pair at +σ ± jω with σ > 0 is the natural mode e+σtcos(ωt). With u = 0, any tiny nonzero initial condition, sensor noise, or disturbance excites it a little, and then it grows exponentially. That's the definition of instability for an LTI system: the free response doesn't die out. So yes, those signals do spiral out of control. Nothing has to drive them.

  • Click the “unstable poles (RHP)” preset (poles at +0.4 ± j2). The ◆ can still sit on the jω axis and G(jω) is still a perfectly good number, but it only describes the forced part. In a real lab sine test the total output = forced sine + free response, and the free response grows like e0.4t. Within seconds it drowns the sine, so the test never settles.
  • That's why a Bode plot of an unstable plant can't be measured open-loop (it can still be computed), and why the first job of feedback is to bulldoze the peaks back into the LHP (M5, root locus in S8).
  • Real hardware doesn't grow forever. It saturates, clips, or breaks. That's the linear model telling you it is no longer valid. Oscillators use exactly this on purpose (M9).
TrapAn RHP zero is not the same thing. A zero isn't a mode, so it never makes the free response grow. It causes a wrong-way start and limits feedback (the “RHP zero” preset; details in M10), but the system can be perfectly stable. Only poles decide stability.
M9

So how do oscillators and amplifiers fit in? Do RHP poles give infinite power?

No infinite power. Where the poles sit tells you what kind of circuit you have. An ideal oscillator has its poles exactly on the jω axis, so its free response rings forever at constant amplitude, cos(ω0t), with no input. A practical oscillator is built with its poles slightly in the RHP so the ring starts by itself from noise and grows. Then nonlinear saturation (clipping at the supply rails, transistor gain compression) lowers the effective loop gain as the amplitude rises. That pushes the effective poles back onto the axis, and the amplitude settles at a finite value. A linear amplifier you want to behave keeps all its poles in the LHP: “high gain” means |G| is large along the jω slice, not that the poles moved right.

TrapDon't equate high gain with RHP poles. Gain is surface height along the jω slice (|G(jω)|). Stability is where the peaks stand. A stable amp can have huge gain with every pole in the LHP. And a pole sitting exactly on the axis is a knife edge: in practice tiny parameter drift tips it to decay or grow, which is why real oscillators aim slightly RHP and let saturation hold the amplitude.
M10

What does a zero in the right half-plane actually do?

It's not a growing mode: zeros aren't modes. A zero at s = +z just means the input shape e+zt gets blocked (G = 0 there). On the surface it is still a sinkhole, only one standing in the RHP. Nothing in the free response grows because of it. What it changes is phase, and through phase, what feedback can do. Systems with RHP zeros are called non-minimum phase.
↩️
Three consequences
  • Same gain, extra lag. Mirror the zero to −z and |G(jω)| doesn't change at all (the distance from any point on the jω axis to +z equals the distance to −z, M7), but the phase picks up extra lag (up to 180° more at high frequency). Same Bode magnitude, worse Bode phase.
  • Wrong-way start. The step response often heads the wrong way first (initial undershoot) before going to its final value: the classic inverse response of a boiler drum, a bicycle steered into a turn, or a time delay's Padé approximation. Try the RHP zero in the s-plane step explorer.
  • A speed limit for feedback. That extra lag eats phase margin. Crank the loop gain to push bandwidth past roughly the zero's frequency z, and the loop rings or goes unstable. The closed-loop poles get pulled toward the RHP zero as gain rises (root locus ends on zeros).
Recognize
  • Click the explorer's “RHP zero (sinkhole in RHP)” preset: the ○ sits right of the jω axis, the surface still dips to 0 there, and the poles (and so stability) are unchanged. The status line flags non-minimum phase.
  • Numerator factor like (1 − s/z) or (z − s) with z > 0, i.e. a sign flip between the s term and the constant: suspect an RHP zero.
  • If complex RHP zeros sit close to the jω axis, the Bode slice still shows a notch there, exactly like LHP zeros would. The magnitude can't tell them apart; the phase can.
TrapDon't confuse an RHP zero with an RHP pole. RHP pole: a mode that grows with no input, so the system is unstable (M8). RHP zero: no mode at all, the system can be perfectly stable, but it starts the wrong way and limits how aggressive your controller can be. Also, you can't cancel an RHP zero with an RHP controller pole: that pole would be an unstable hidden mode.
M1

So what is a point s = σ + jω, in plain words?

It's an address that names a type of exponential signal, est = eσt(cos ωt + j sin ωt): σ says how fast it grows or decays, and ω how fast it oscillates. G(s) at that address is the complex number the system would multiply that signal by. The point itself is neither a state of the system nor a setting.

Linear systems have a special property: feed in est and (after transients) you get back G(s)·est, the same shape, just scaled and rotated. So the plane is a catalogue of test shapes, and the surface records how the system treats each one. On the jω axis (σ = 0) the shapes are steady sines. Left of it (σ < 0) they're decaying sines; right of it (σ > 0), growing ones.

RecognizeWhen someone says “evaluate at s = j2,” read it as “put the ◆ at j2 and read the surface height and phase there.” You get a gain and a phase for a 2 rad/s sine. Nothing moves.
M2

Is my spot on the plane the input?

No. The input is a time signal, e.g. 3 sin(2t). Your spot (s = j2) is only the label that says “a 2 rad/s sine,” i.e. where to look up the answer. The amplitude 3 isn't on the map at all. The output is 3·|G(j2)|·sin(2t + ∠G(j2)), a time signal, not another point.

M3

Does the gradient pull me toward a pole? Does the system “slide” across the plane over time?

No. There's no ball rolling on this surface. For a fixed system the surface is frozen, and your lookup spot is wherever you put it (the drive frequency). The only motion on the map is poles moving when you turn a knob, and that's a design change, not something the response does in time.

The slope of the surface still means something. A steep rise as you move up the jω axis toward a peak means the gain changes quickly with frequency (a sharp resonance). But slope is information about the system, not a force acting on anything.

TrapDon't confuse root-locus motion (closed-loop poles travel as you sweep the gain K, S8) with time evolution. A root locus is a flip-book of different surfaces, one per K, not one system moving.
M4

Why does a sine input only use the jω axis?

A steady sine neither grows nor decays, so its σ = 0. sin ωt = (ejωt − e−jωt)/2j, which lives at exactly s = ±jω. Those are two mirror-image points on the jω axis (the surface is symmetric, so reading one is enough).
Recognize
  • Bode plot = a slice of the |G| surface along the jω axis: |G(jω)| and ∠G(jω) for ω from small to large.
  • Poles are not on the jω axis for a stable system. They sit in the LHP. Their effect on a sine test is felt only through how high they lift the surface along the slice: a peak close to the axis gives a resonant bump at that ω.
  • Off-axis addresses still matter, because that's where the poles live. They describe the system's own free motions (decaying sines at −σ ± jω), not something you drive it with in a frequency test. If a pole sits in the RHP, that free motion grows instead, with or without your sine (M8).
M5

Then what does turning a control knob (like gain K) do on the map?

It rebuilds the surface: the closed-loop poles (peaks) move, so the height everywhere changes, including under your fixed lookup spot. In the explorer, slide the pole pair toward the jω axis (−a → 0) or up (larger b) and watch the readout at a fixed ◆ probe change: the bulldozer moved the peak, not you. (In a feedback loop, a gain knob moves the closed-loop poles the same way. See the root locus in S8.)
The two-hand ruleOne hand holds the bulldozer (knobs → where the peaks are). The other hand holds the ◆ probe (drive frequency → where you read). They're independent. Design = use the bulldozer so that, along the stretch of the jω axis your signals actually use, the height and phase of the slice are what you want: high and flat at low frequency (good tracking) and low at high frequency (noise rejection), just like S and C.

Same as a filter: the component values set the pole/zero map (the surface). The signal generator's frequency is where you probe it. Turning the generator's dial never moves the poles.

S2

Intuitively, what is the difference between a pole and a zero?

Poles belong to the system: they are its natural modes, the motions it makes on its own (the free response). Zeros belong to the input→output path: they decide how strongly the input excites each mode and how strongly each mode shows up at the output. Poles set which exponentials appear; zeros set how much of each.

Non-minimum phase: the wrong-way start

A zero in the RHP (e.g. G = (1 − s)/((s+1)(s+2))·2) makes the step response start in the wrong direction before heading to the correct final value. Examples: a boiler's water level briefly rises when you add cold water (shrink/swell), and an aircraft briefly dips when the elevator commands a climb. Feedback can't fix this with high gain. A RHP zero attracts closed-loop poles into the RHP as gain rises, so it limits how fast you can make the loop.

Pole–zero cancellation

If a ○ lands exactly on a ×, that mode disappears from the input→output transfer function, but it still exists inside the system. The input can't excite it, or the output can't see it. An initial condition or a disturbance entering elsewhere can still excite it. Cancelling a stable, fast pole is harmless. “Cancelling” an unstable pole hides an internal instability that will still blow up (Q20).

Trap
  • A zero doesn't make a system faster or slower in the sense of new modes. It only reweights existing modes. Still, a zero near the dominant poles can drastically change overshoot (the boring-machine PD zero, Q38).
  • A near-cancellation (○ close to ×) leaves a small residue: a slow, small “tail” that can still matter for settling time.

S7

What do the final- and initial-value theorems mean on the map?

FVT: look at s → 0 (the origin, i.e. DC): y(∞) = lims→0 sY(s), valid only if every × of sY(s) is in the open LHP. IVT: look at s → ∞ (far away, i.e. infinitely high frequency): y(0⁺) = lims→∞ sY(s), which is set by the relative degree (#×'s − #○'s).
Recognize
  • FVT is a statement about the neighborhood of the origin: DC gain, integrators (× at 0 → ∞ gain), differentiators (○ at 0 → zero gain).
  • The validity check is a statement about the whole plane: any × of sY on the jω axis or in the RHP ⇒ no final value (Q27 trap).
  • IVT: step response of a strictly proper G starts at y(0⁺) = 0. Relative degree 1 ⇒ it starts with a nonzero slope; relative degree 2 ⇒ it starts flat. Far from all the marks, G looks like 1/s(relative degree).
S8

What does closing the loop do on the map? (root-locus preview)

Closed-loop poles are the roots of 1 + K·L(s) = 0. As gain K goes from 0 to ∞, they start at the open-loop ×'s and travel toward the open-loop ○'s (or off to infinity). Open-loop zeros stay put. The path they trace is the root locus, which is a later topic. For now: feedback moves poles, and the open-loop pattern decides where they can go.

Root-locus preview: L(s) = K / (s(s + 2))  →  closed loop s² + 2s + K = 0

Recognize
  • For K/(s(s+2)): the poles start at 0 and −2, meet at −1 when K = 1 (critical damping), then split vertically. More gain gives more ωn but less ζ, with σ stuck at −1. That's the boring machine's ζ = 6/√K story in pictures.
  • Adding a LHP zero bends the locus left toward the ○: more damping, better stability. That's why PD/lead control helps. A RHP zero would pull poles into the RHP.
  • This one picture ties together Stage 4 (one loop, one characteristic equation) and Stage 13 (high gain trades damping).

Q5

What exactly is G(s)?

G(s) = Y(s)/U(s) with zero initial conditions: output transform over input transform. For lumped LTI systems it's a ratio of polynomials in s.
G(s) = Y(s)U(s) = bmsm + … + b1s + b0sn + an−1sn−1 + … + a1s + a0

Q9

When stores aren't independent (two capacitors in parallel act as one), or when a pole–zero cancellation hides a mode from the transfer function. Count poles of the final G(s), not parts on the schematic.

Q10

What does the standard feedback loop look like, and what is C/R?

Reference R minus measured output gives error E. The controller Gc turns E into actuation, the plant G produces output C (or Y), and a disturbance Td enters along the way. With unity feedback, C/R = GcG/(1 + GcG).
CR = GcG1 + GcG     CTd = G1 + GcG
Q11

How do I get C/D (output due to the disturbance)?

Set R = 0 and redraw with Td as the input. The forward path from Td to C is just G, and the loop is unchanged, so C/Td = G/(1 + GcG).

Rule of thumb for any input: numerator = forward path from that input to the output; denominator = 1 + loop gain. That's why C/R and C/Td share a denominator. With both inputs present, superposition gives C = [GcG·R + G·Td]/(1 + GcG).

Recognize
  • Same characteristic equation 1 + GcG = 0 for every input. Stability and the transient “personality” (ζ, ωn) belong to the loop, not to the input.
  • Only the numerator changes with the input, so the final value and the zeros differ.
Q12

Why does it matter where the disturbance enters?

It changes the numerator. A disturbance entering at the plant input is filtered by G, giving G/(1+L). One at the plant output gives 1/(1+L). In both cases, making the loop gain large where the disturbance lives is what suppresses it.
PredictGc = K (P only), G = 1/(s+1), unit-step disturbance at the plant input, R = 0. Where does C settle?
ShowFVT: C(∞) = lim s·1/(s+1)1 + K/(s+1)·1s = 1/(1+K). Bigger K shrinks the effect but never removes it. For that you need integral action in Gc (Stage 12).
Key takeawayHigh loop gain helps reject disturbances, and an integrator in the controller, upstream of where the disturbance enters drives the steady-state effect of a constant disturbance to zero.

Q15

We already have transfer functions. Why state space?

Transfer functions only see input → output from rest. State space keeps the internal variables, handles initial conditions and multiple inputs/outputs (MIMO), is how computers simulate, and is the language of modern control (state feedback, observers, optimal control).
Why it matters
  • Initial conditions: x(0) is built in (compare Q3).
  • MIMO: two inputs and three outputs is just bigger B and C, not a 3×2 grid of transfer functions.
  • Simulation: ODE solvers (and Simulink) integrate first-order vector equations.
  • Hidden modes: internal states that a pole–zero cancellation would hide stay visible.
Q16

Q17

  3. Write ẋi for each state using only states and u. The coefficients form A, and the u-coefficients form B.dynamics
  4. Write y in terms of states and u. The coefficients form C and D.measurement

10 · Dominant poles

📍
Where are we in the big picture?Real systems have many poles, but the response is usually shaped by just one or two. This is why the first- and second-order formulas are so useful, and it sets up model simplification in Lecture Notes 2.
Describe→Loop→State space→Response→SSE→Feedback
Q24

Why do some poles matter more than others?

Each pole contributes a mode ept. Poles far to the left die out fast; the poles closest to the imaginary axis are still alive when everything else is gone, so they dominate what you see. If a pole is roughly 5–10× farther left than the dominant ones, you can usually drop it.

A pole at −30 decays 15× faster than one at −2. By the time the slow mode has done anything visible, the fast one has disappeared. That's why design often uses a first- or second-order approximation and why the specs in Stage 11 are defined for the second-order template.

Dominant-pole explorer: second-order pair + one extra real pole

Full: G = pωn²(s²+2ζωns+ωn²)(s+p) vs. reduced: ωn²s²+2ζωns+ωn² (same DC gain = 1). ωn = 1, ζ = 0.5.

Recognize
  • Pole ratio ≥ ~5 → the extra pole barely matters. Ratio near 1 → it's no longer negligible (slower, more sluggish response).
  • When you drop a pole, keep the DC gain: K/(s(s+2)(s+30)) ≈ (K/30)/(s(s+2)), because (s+30) ≈ 30 at low frequency (Lecture Notes 2).
  • A zero near a pole nearly cancels it, which also removes that pole's influence.

What is the standard form, and what do ζ and ωn mean?

12 · Steady-state error: P vs I

📍
Where are we in the big picture?Transient analysis tells you how the response moves; this tells you where it ends up. The professor emphasized it: why P alone leaves an error and why I removes it.
Describe→Loop→State space→Response→SSE→Feedback
Q27

How do I compute steady-state error?

With unity feedback, E(s) = R(s)/(1 + L(s)). The final-value theorem gives ess = lims→0 sE(s), but only if the closed loop is stable.
ess = lims→0 s · 11 + Gc(s)G(s) · R(s)   →   unit step: ess = 11 + L(0)
Trap: an unstable loop has no steady stateThe final-value theorem will still happily give you a number for an unstable closed loop, and that number is meaningless. Always check stability (all closed-loop poles in the LHP) first.
Q28

Why does pure P leave an error, and why does I remove it?

P: u = Kpe. To hold the output at a nonzero value, a type-0 plant needs a nonzero u, which means a nonzero e. So ess = 1/(1 + KpG(0)), which shrinks with gain but never reaches zero. I: in steady state every signal is constant, including the integrator's output. An integrator's output is constant only if its input is zero, so e = 0.

In transfer-function terms, the integrator puts a pole at s = 0 in L, so L(0) = ∞ and 1/(1+L(0)) = 0. The plant can supply that integrator itself (a type-1 plant like 1/(s(s+1)) in the boring machine). That's why the boring machine has zero step error even with a PD controller.

Q33

Open loop vs closed loop: what changes when you add a sensor?

Open loop applies a pre-computed input and hopes. It can't notice a disturbance or a plant change. Closed loop measures the output (sensor H), compares it with the reference, and acts on the error. The costs: a sensor, sensor noise N that now enters the loop, and the possibility of instability.
Why it mattersEvery advantage below (sensitivity, disturbance rejection, faster transients, less steady-state error) exists only because of the loop. Every disadvantage (noise, more hardware, possible instability) exists for the same reason.
Q34

Error-signal analysis: what are S and C, and why does S + C = 1 matter?

With H = 1 and L = GcG: E = SR − SGTd + CN, where sensitivity S = 1/(1+L) and complementary sensitivity C = L/(1+L). Small S means good tracking and disturbance rejection; small C means good noise rejection. But S + C = 1, so you can't make both small at the same frequency.
E(s) = 11 + LR − G1 + LTd + L1 + LN     S + C = 1 + L1 + L = 1

The way out is frequency separation. References and disturbances are usually slow, while sensor noise is fast. So design L to be large at low frequency (S small there) and small at high frequency (C small there). The crossover where |L| ≈ 1 is the loop bandwidth.

|S| and |C| vs frequency for L(s) = K/(s(s+1))

Raise K: S drops at low frequency (better tracking) but C stays near 1 out to higher frequency (more noise passes), and a resonant peak shows the loop getting less damped.

It's a PLL loop filter. Inside the loop bandwidth the output follows the reference (and its noise); outside it, the VCO's own noise wins. Same S/C trade-off.

Trap: which C?In these slides, C(s) = L/(1+L) is the complementary sensitivity, which for unity feedback equals the closed-loop transfer function T(s) = Y/R. It's not the state-space output matrix C, and not the output signal C in C/R.
Q35

Sensitivity to parameter variations: why does feedback make a sloppy plant behave?

System sensitivity STG = (∂T/T)/(∂G/G), i.e. the % change in the closed-loop transfer function per % change in the plant. Open loop: 1 (a 10% plant drift gives a 10% output error). Closed loop: 1/(1 + GcG), so large loop gain makes the plant's drift almost irrelevant.
STG = ∂T∂G·GT = 11 + Gc(s)G(s)    with T = GcG1 + GcG
PredictGcG = 100 at low frequency, and the plant gain drops 10% as it heats up. How much does the closed-loop gain change?
ShowAbout 10% × 1/101 ≈ 0.1%. The closed loop is ~100× less sensitive than the open loop.

This is Black's feedback amplifier (1927): an op-amp's gain varies wildly from part to part, but the closed-loop gain ≈ 1/β is set by precise resistors. Same 1/(1+L).

RecognizeParameter sensitivity STG is the same function as the S in the error equation. Errors, plant changes, and disturbances are all divided by (1 + L). The slides also define sensitivity to a parameter α inside G: STα = STG·SGα (chain rule).
Q36

Disturbance rejection: what does the steel-mill example show?

With R = N = 0, E = −SGTd = −G1+LTd. In the steel rolling mill, the load torque from the rolls slows the motor. Closing the loop with a tachometer cuts the steady-state speed drop by the loop gain, typically to under 2% of the open-loop drop.
Steady-state speed change for load step Td = D/s
Open loop|Δω| = D / (b + KmKb/Ra)
Closed loop (tachometer Kt, amplifier Ka)|Δω| ≈ RaD / (KaKmKt)
Ratio closed/open(Rab + KmKb)/(KaKmKt), usually < 0.02

Intuition: in open loop, only the motor's own friction and back-EMF resist the load. In closed loop, the amplifier sees the speed drop and pushes back with gain Ka. Large G1G2H ⇒ E ≈ Td/(G1H).

Noise is the flip side (same slide set)With R = Td = 0, Y = −C·N ≈ −N when L is large. The loop trusts the sensor completely, so it faithfully “corrects” the output to follow the noise. Attenuating measurement noise needs small loop gain at the noise frequencies.
Q37

Control of the transient response: how does feedback make a slow motor fast?

The open-loop motor speed TF is K1/(τ1s + 1), and its speed is fixed by the hardware (the steel mill's huge roll inertia makes τ1 large). Closing the loop moves the pole from −1/τ1 to −(1 + KaKtK1)/τ1, so the response is (1 + loop gain)× faster without changing the motor.

Q38

Steady-state error design: what's the trade-off in the English Channel boring machine?

PD controller Gc = K + 11s on plant G = 1/(s(s+1)). One gain K must keep the angle response to commands acceptable and suppress the cutter's load disturbance. Step-reference error is 0 for any K (type-1 plant), but a step disturbance leaves yss = 1/K: 0.01 at K = 100, 0.05 at K = 20.

Recognize
  • Same denominator s² + 12s + K for both inputs (Q11 again). ωn = √K, ζ = 6/√K, so a bigger K is less damped.
  • The R-path numerator K + 11s is a zero at −K/11. That's why K = 20 (ζ > 1, overdamped poles!) still overshoots: the zero does it (Q26 trap).
  • The disturbance enters after the controller, and the controller has no integrator, so the disturbance error only shrinks as 1/K.
Design readingBigger K buys disturbance rejection (1/K) and speed (ωn = √K) at the cost of less damping (ζ = 6/√K) and more actuator effort. Choosing K is choosing where to sit on that trade-off.
Q39

Performance indices: why ISE, IAE, ITAE, ITSE?

A performance index squashes a whole error curve into one number so you can optimize a gain. ISE = ∫e² dt punishes big errors (mostly the early ones). IAE = ∫|e|dt weighs all errors equally. ITAE = ∫t|e|dt and ITSE = ∫te² dt multiply by time, so errors that linger late get punished, which favors well-damped responses.
Slide example: space telescope pointingChoose K3 to minimize a disturbance's effect: min ISE at K3 = √10 ≈ 3.2 (ζ = 0.50), min IAE at K3 = 4.2 (ζ = 0.665). Different index, different “optimal” design, which is the point: the index is how you encode what you care about.
Recognize“Optimal” always means “optimal for some index.” The time-weighted (T-) versions are what you choose when settling matters more than the first instant.
Q40

Simplification of linear systems: when and how can I use a smaller model?

Keep the dominant dynamics and the DC gain; drop or approximate the rest. The simple way: delete insignificant (fast) poles while keeping G(0). The refined way (slides): pick a lower-order GL whose frequency response matches the original's near s = 0, by matching derivatives.

Slide example: GH = 6/((s+1)(s+2)(s+3)) → GL = 1.60/(s² + 2.590s + 1.60). Both have DC gain 1. The reduced poles (−1.024, −1.565) give a slightly overdamped response with Ts ≈ 3 s.

TrapDropping a pole without fixing the gain: 6/((s+1)(s+2)) has DC gain 3, not 1. Divide by the dropped pole's constant (here 3) to keep G(0).

★ “Recognize this” cheat sheet

Reading a transfer function

  • Order = highest power of s in the denominator
  • DC gain = G(0); unit step settles there (if stable)
  • b/(s+a) → K = b/a, τ = 1/a
  • Bare 1/s → integrator → type 1
  • Poles: Re → decay, Im → ringing, RHP → unstable

Block diagrams

  • Series ×, parallel +, loop G/(1+GH)
  • Any input: forward path / (1 + L)
  • C/R and C/D share 1 + GcG → same poles
  • Disturbance at plant input: G/(1+L)

Order & energy

  • Order = # independent L, C, m, k, J
  • R and dampers add no order
  • Parallel C's = one store; cancellations hide modes

State space

  • ẋ = Ax + Bu, y = Cx + Du
  • Physics → states → A, B; output choice → C, D
  • Phase variables: 1's above the diagonal, −a0 −a1… in the bottom row
  • G = C(sI−A)−1B + D; poles = eig(A)
  • 2×2 inverse: swap diag, negate off, ÷ det

Circuits & op-amps

  • Impedances R, sL, 1/(sC)
  • RLC: 1/(LCs²+RCs+1)
  • Op-amp: −Zf/Zin
  • Pole at 0 = integrator; zero at 0 = differentiator

First order K/(Ts+1)

  • 63% at T, 98% at 4T
  • No overshoot, ever
  • Initial slope K/T; impulse (K/T)e−t/T

Second order

  • ωn²/(s²+2ζωns+ωn²)
  • %OS = 100e−ζπ/√(1−ζ²) (ζ only)
  • Ts ≈ 4/(ζωn), Tp = π/ωd
  • cos θ = ζ; radius = ωn
  • Zeros add overshoot; extra poles slow it down

Steady state

  • ess = lim sE(s), stable loop only!
  • Step, type 0: 1/(1+L(0))
  • Integrator in L → step error 0
  • P leaves error; I removes it; D doesn't affect it

Feedback (LN2)

  • S = 1/(1+L), C = L/(1+L), S+C = 1
  • E = SR − SGTd + CN
  • Open-loop sensitivity 1; closed-loop 1/(1+L)
  • Feedback: τ → τ/(1+loop gain)
  • Boring machine: yss(Td) = 1/K
  • ISE ↔ big errors; ITAE ↔ late errors
  • Reduce order: keep DC gain
