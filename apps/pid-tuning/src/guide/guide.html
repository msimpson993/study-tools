Q1

What problem does feedback control actually solve?

Making a physical quantity (speed, temperature, position, voltage) follow a desired value automatically, even though the system is imperfectly known and is being pushed around by disturbances.

Think of cruise control, or exactly your project: engine speed control. You set a desired speed r (the reference, or set-point). The engine (the plant) produces a speed y. The only thing you can touch is the throttle/actuator command u. Meanwhile, the world adds disturbances d: a hill, a load torque from the alternator or A/C compressor, friction that changes as the engine warms up.

So the job is: choose u(t) so that y(t) ≈ r(t), quickly and smoothly, without knowing the disturbances in advance and with only an approximate model of the engine.

RF analogy (you'll like this)A phase-locked loop or an automatic gain control (AGC) loop is the same problem: a reference, a measured output, an error detector, and a loop filter that decides the correction. A PLL's loop filter is often literally a PI controller, and you've already met loop bandwidth vs. phase margin trade-offs there. Same math, different hardware.

Q2

Why use feedback instead of just computing the right throttle (open loop)?

Open loop only works if you know the plant exactly and nothing disturbs it. Feedback measures the result and corrects for whatever went wrong, whether that's model error or disturbances, without needing to know why.

Open loop: “1200 rpm needs 18% throttle, so command 18%.” That fails as soon as the load changes or the engine is warmer than the day you calibrated. Closed loop: measure y, form the error e = r − y, and let a controller C(s) turn the error into a command.

From class: with forward path CG and unity feedback, superposition gives two transfer functions with the same denominator:

YR = C G1 + C G      YD = G1 + C G      ER = 11 + C G

Read them like an engineer: wherever the loop gain |CG| is large, Y/R → 1 (good tracking) and Y/D → 0 (disturbance rejected). That one sentence is the reason feedback works, and it's the seed of why integral action (infinite gain at DC) gives zero steady-state error.

Q3

So what goes wrong with feedback?

Feedback acts on delayed, lagged information. Push too hard and you overshoot or oscillate; push too gently and you're slow or leave a permanent offset. Noise and actuator limits add their own problems.

Q4

What is a PID controller, and why are P, I and D each there?

It adds three reactions to the error: P reacts to the error now (present), I to the error accumulated so far (past), D to how fast the error is changing (a prediction of the near future).
Looks atIntuition (driving)Good atBad at / risk
P  KpePresent error“I'm 10 mph slow → press harder, in proportion.”Fast basic correctionLeaves offset on type-0 plants; too much ⇒ oscillation
I  Ki∫e dtPast (accumulated) error“I've been slow for a while → keep adding throttle until the error is truly gone.”Removes steady-state error; rejects constant loadsAdds lag ⇒ overshoot; windup when saturated
D  Kd de/dtFuture trend“I'm closing in fast → ease off before I overshoot.”Damping, less overshootAmplifies noise; “kick” on set-point steps

Q5

Why write it as u = Kpe + Ki∫e + Kd de/dt, and what's its transfer function?

The time-domain law is literally “present + past + future.” Laplace-transform it (zero ICs) and integration becomes 1/s and differentiation becomes s, so it drops straight into block-diagram algebra: C(s) = Kp + Ki/s + Kds.
u(t) = Kp e(t) + Ki ∫0t e(τ) dτ + Kd dedt   ⟹   U(s)E(s) = Kp + Kis + Kds = Kds² + Kps + Kis

Reading the transfer function like a math person:

  • One pole at the origin (from 1/s). That pole is the whole story of zero steady-state error (Deep dive A).
  • Two zeros (roots of Kds² + Kps + Ki). Tuning is really placing these two zeros and setting an overall gain. A PI has one zero at s = −Ki/Kp = −1/Ti.
  • Ideal D is improper (more zeros than poles; gain → ∞ as ω → ∞). Real implementations add a filter: Kds → KdN ss + N. This is exactly the N (“filter coefficient”) field in the Simulink PID block (Deep dive B).

Special cases you've seen in class: P (Ki = Kd = 0), PI (Kd = 0), PD (Ki = 0), I-only (Kp = Kd = 0). Hold on to I-only. Your Project 1 tuner lands on it at one point.

Q6

Parallel form vs. standard (ideal) form: what's the difference, and how do I convert?

Same controller, different parameters. Standard form factors out Kp: C = Kp(1 + 1/(Tis) + Tds). Convert with Ki = Kp/Ti and Kd = KpTd.
Parallel: Kp + Kis + Kds    ⟺    Standard (your class): Kp(1 + 1Tis + Tds)

Trap“Increase Ti to get more integral action” is wrong: Ti is in the denominator. And in Simulink's Ideal form the “I” field is 1/Ti, not Ti. Always check which form a dialog is in before you copy numbers.
Quick checkKp = 2, Ti = 0.5 s, Td = 0.1 s ⇒ Ki = 2/0.5 = 4 s⁻¹, Kd = 2 × 0.1 = 0.2 s. Physical meaning of Ti: for a constant error, the I term needs Ti seconds to add as much as the P term already contributes.

Q7

What does increasing each gain do to the step response?

Roughly: Kp makes it faster but more oscillatory and only reduces offset; Ki eliminates offset but adds overshoot and slows settling; Kd adds damping (less overshoot, faster settling) but amplifies noise.
Increase…Rise timeOvershootSettling timeSteady-state errorStability margin
Kp↓ decreases↑ increasessmall change↓ decreases (not to 0)↓ degrades
Ki↓ decreases↑ increases↑ increaseseliminated↓ degrades
Kdminor change↓ decreases↓ decreasesno effect (in theory)↑ improves (if Kd modest)
These are rules of thumb, not theoremsThe table describes the typical trend near a reasonable design. Push any gain far enough and the trend can reverse. Too much Kd can make things worse, and with noise it always costs actuator jitter. Use the table to predict, then confirm with simulation, which is exactly the habit your instructor wants.

A first-order sanity check (ties to your class). With a first-order plant G = K/(τs+1) and P control, the closed loop is still first order:

YR = KpKτs + 1 + KpK = KpK/(1+KpK)(τ/(1+KpK)) s + 1

So raising Kp shrinks the time constant by (1 + KpK) (faster) and pushes the DC gain toward 1 (smaller offset) but never to exactly 1. Example: τ = 1, K = 1, Kp = 3 ⇒ time constant 0.25 s, final value 0.75, error 0.25. Overshoot and oscillation only appear when the closed loop is second order or higher, which is why the next class topic (ζ, ωn) is the natural language for tuning.

Deep dive A

Why does integral action give zero steady-state error?

🧭
Mini big picture for this detailThis explains row 2 of the symptoms table (“offset”) and the Project 1 requirement “zero steady-state error to a step”. Three ways to see the same fact:
Equilibrium argument: an integrator's output can only stop changing if its input e is zero.
Final value theorem: ess = 1/(1 + C(0)G(0)), and an integrator makes C(0) = ∞.
Disturbances: the same infinite DC gain wipes out constant loads.
Fine print: only if the closed loop is stable. And it costs phase lag.

1. The equilibrium argument (the intuitive one)

Suppose the loop settles to steady state. Then every signal is constant, including the integrator output xi = ∫e dt. But dxi/dt = e. A constant xi requires e = 0. There is no other way for the loop to sit still. P-only control has no such constraint: it can sit still at any e that makes Kpe equal to the needed throttle, which is why it needs an offset to “hold” the throttle open.

2. The final value theorem (the one for exams)

Unity feedback, step reference R(s) = 1/s, closed loop stable:

ess = lims→0 s · 11 + C(s)G(s) · 1s = 11 + C(0)G(0)
  • P: ess = 1/(1 + KpG(0)). Finite Kp ⇒ finite but nonzero error (for a plant with no free integrator, i.e. “type 0”).
  • PI/PID: C(s) ∋ Ki/s ⇒ C(0) → ∞ ⇒ ess = 0.
  • If the plant itself has an integrator (type 1, e.g. a position servo), P alone already gives zero step error, and I is then for rejecting constant disturbances.

3. Disturbances: the C/D view from class

YD(0) = G(0)1 + C(0)G(0)  →  G(0)1 + KpG(0) (P)   or   0 (with I)
Fine print(i) Zero error requires the closed loop to be stable; the final value theorem is invalid otherwise. (ii) The integrator contributes −90° of phase at all frequencies, which is why too much Ki adds overshoot and can destabilize. (iii) “Zero error” is for step references/constant disturbances; a ramp reference with a type-1 loop still leaves a finite error.
Deep dive B

The derivative term: damping, noise, filters and “kick”

🧭
Mini big picture for this detailD is the “future” term. It's powerful for damping, but it is the term most likely to bite you in practice. And your Project 1 controller is PI, so understanding why D is often left out is part of the story.
Why it damps: opposes fast motion, like a shock absorber.
Why it's noisy: |Kdjω| grows with frequency.
Fix 1: filter KdNs/(s+N) caps the HF gain at KdN.
Fix 2: D on measurement avoids the set-point “kick”.

Why it damps

Near the target the error is small but changing fast; D sees the slope and starts braking early. It's a velocity-proportional force, a shock absorber, so it adds damping (raises ζ, once you meet ζ next class). In frequency terms it adds phase lead (up to +90°) near crossover, which increases phase margin.

Why it amplifies noise

Your RF intuition is exactly right here: the ideal differentiator's frequency response is Kd·jω, a +20 dB/decade high-pass with unbounded gain. Sensor noise lives at high frequency, so D turns a tiny noise ripple into a large actuator chatter. Example: noise at 100 rad/s with Kd = 1 gets gain 100.

Filtered D:  Kd N ss + N  ≈  Kds for ω ≪ N,    → KdN for ω ≫ N

Derivative kick

If D acts on the error e = r − y and r steps, de/dt is an impulse, so u gets a huge spike (limited only by N). Common fix: apply D to −y only (“derivative on measurement”). For a constant r it's identical, but it ignores set-point jumps. Try the “D on measurement” toggle in the Lab and watch the peak |u| readout.

Why a PI (not PID) is a sensible choice for Project 1The example asks for a PI controller. That makes sense: the requirements (settle < 5 s, zero step error) need the I term, and in your project they can be met without D. Leaving D out sidesteps the noise and kick issues entirely. A common practical default is PI unless you really need the extra damping.

Q8

What does “tuning” mean, and why is it hard?

Tuning = picking Kp, Ki, Kd (and filter N) so the loop meets specs such as settling time, overshoot, steady-state error and robustness. It's hard because the specs conflict: faster usually means less robust, noisier and harder on the actuator.

The core trade-offs:

  • Speed vs robustness. A faster loop needs higher gain at higher frequencies, where the plant's phase lag is larger, so less phase margin, more overshoot, and more sensitivity to model error.
  • Speed vs noise. Higher bandwidth lets more sensor noise through to the actuator (D makes it worse).
  • Speed vs actuator effort. Fast responses demand big, sharp control signals. Real actuators saturate, which leads to windup.
  • Tracking vs disturbance rejection. A tuning optimized for set-point steps isn't automatically best for load disturbances.
Q9

How do we measure “good”? (A gentle preview of second-order systems)

With step-response numbers: rise time, overshoot (%), settling time (time to stay within ±2% of final value), and steady-state error. For loops that behave like a second-order system, two numbers, the damping ratio ζ and natural frequency ωn, predict all of them.

Many closed loops (including a PI on a first-order plant, or an I-only loop) are exactly second order, and higher-order loops are often dominated by one pair of complex poles. The standard form is:

Q10

How do you tune by hand?

Start with P, add I to kill the offset, add D only if you need damping. Change one gain at a time, by factors (×1.5 or ×2), and predict the effect before each run.
  1. P first. Set Ki = Kd = 0. Raise Kp until the response is reasonably fast with mild oscillation. ↑Kp: faster, more OS, less offset
  2. Add I. Increase Ki (decrease Ti) until the offset disappears in acceptable time. Back off if overshoot grows. I ⇒ ess = 0, adds lag
  3. Add D if needed. Increase Kd to reduce overshoot/ringing; stop when noise in u becomes unacceptable; keep a filter. D ⇒ damping, noise
  4. Iterate & check margins. Re-tweak Kp; test with a disturbance and with the actuator limit; verify the spec numbers. robustness
Deep dive C

Ziegler–Nichols: tuning from the edge of instability

🧭
Mini big picture for this detailA historical, model-free recipe (1942). The idea is to find where the loop just oscillates, then back off by fixed ratios. Useful as a quick start, and a favourite exam calculation.
P only, raise Kp until sustained oscillation.
Record Ku (ultimate gain) and Tu (period).
Look up gains in the table (standard form).
Expect ~quarter-decay, i.e. fairly big overshoot, so detune.
Controller (closed-loop / ultimate-gain method)KpTiTd

Worked example (the Lab's plant 1/(s+1)³). With P control the characteristic equation is (s+1)³ + Kp = s³ + 3s² + 3s + (1 + Kp) = 0. Routh–Hurwitz (or substitute s = jω): marginal when 3·3 = 1 + Kp, so Ku = 8, with oscillation at ωu = √3 rad/s, so Tu = 2π/√3 ≈ 3.63 s. Classic ZN PID: Kp = 4.8, Ti = 1.81 s, Td = 0.45 s ⇒ Ki = Kp/Ti ≈ 2.65, Kd = KpTd ≈ 2.18.

The other ZN method (open-loop “reaction curve”)Apply a step to the plant (no feedback), fit an S-curve with gain K, dead time L and time constant T, then e.g. PID: Kp = 1.2T/(KL), Ti = 2L, Td = 0.5L. Same spirit, same aggressiveness.
CaveatsDriving a real engine into sustained oscillation can be unsafe. ZN rules are in the standard form, so convert before typing them into a parallel-form block. And ZN gains are a starting point, not an answer.
Q11

What does automatic tuning (MATLAB's PID Tuner) actually do?

It linearizes your Simulink plant at the operating point, then computes gains that achieve a target speed (response time ≈ bandwidth) with a target robustness (transient behavior ≈ phase margin). You steer with two sliders instead of three gains.
  1. Linearize. Opening the tuner from the PID block makes Simulink compute a linear plant model seen by the controller at the current operating point. transfer function of the plant
  2. Design. It picks P, I (and D, N if present) so the open-loop L = C·G crosses 0 dB at a chosen frequency ωc with a chosen phase margin. Its initial design aims at a balance between performance and robustness. loop shaping
  3. Adjust. Time-domain mode: Response time slider (Slower ↔ Faster) and Transient behavior slider (Aggressive ↔ Robust). Frequency-domain mode: Bandwidth and Phase margin. Same two degrees of freedom, different labels. trade-off map
  4. Inspect. “Show parameters” lists gains plus rise/settling time, overshoot, gain & phase margins, closed-loop stability. specs
  5. Update Block writes the gains to the Simulink block; then simulate the nonlinear model. verification
Deep dive D

How the PID Tuner sliders map to bandwidth and phase margin

🧭
Mini big picture for this detailThis is the bridge between the trade-off map (Q8) and your Project 1 slider moves. Two numbers describe a loop's “personality”: how fast (crossover/bandwidth) and how calm (phase margin).
Response time ↕ bandwidth: roughly inversely related.
Transient behavior ↕ phase margin: more robust = more PM.
PM ↔ overshoot: more PM ⇒ less overshoot (ζ ≈ PM/100).
The catch: faster ⇒ more plant lag at ωc ⇒ PM shrinks unless you ask for it back.

Two loop numbers, in your RF language

  • Gain crossover ωc: where |L(jω)| = 1 (0 dB). Closed-loop bandwidth is close to ωc, and response time scales roughly like 1/ωc (double ωc → about half the response time). The Response time slider moves ωc: sliding left = slower = longer response time = lower bandwidth.
  • Phase margin PM = 180° + ∠L(jωc): how far the loop is from the −180° “oscillation condition” at crossover. The Transient behavior slider trades PM: toward Robust = more PM = less overshoot, toward Aggressive = less PM, snappier but more overshoot. (MATLAB's pidtune defaults to a 60° PM target.)
  • Rule of thumb (derived from the standard 2nd-order loop ωn²/(s(s+2ζωn)); only approximate for other loops): ζ ≈ PM/100 for PM ≲ 65°. So PM 45° → ζ ≈ 0.45 → ~20% OS; PM 60° → ζ ≈ 0.6 → ~10% OS; PM ≳ 70° → little or no overshoot.
The sentence to remember“Faster” alone moves you right on the trade-off map and down in PM, so overshoot rises. To get faster and calmer you must also slide toward Robust, if the plant allows it. That is exactly your Project 1's final design.
Deep dive E

Actuator saturation and integrator windup

🧭
Mini big picture for this detailLinear design (what PID Tuner does) assumes the actuator can deliver any u. A throttle can't open past 100%. When it saturates, the I term misbehaves. This is one reason to verify on the nonlinear model.
Saturation: u hits its limit, so the loop is effectively open.
Windup: e stays large, so ∫e keeps growing.
Unwind: integrator must discharge, causing big overshoot and slow settling.
Anti-windup: stop (clamp) or bleed (back-calculate) the integrator.

In Simulink's PID block, anti-windup lives on the Output Saturation tab: enable limits, then choose “clamping” or “back-calculation.”

Q12

What happens at each step of the example, and which concept explains it?

  1. Open the model. open_system('scdspeedctrlpidblock'). Find the loop: Speed Reference step → error → PID Controller block → engine dynamics → measured speed fed back. Q2 block diagram, Y/R = CG/(1+CG)
  2. Read the requirements. (a) settling time < 5 s; (b) zero steady-state error to the step reference. Requirement (b) tells you immediately that you need integral action, which is why the block is configured as PI. Deep dive A
  3. Open PID Tuner (double-click the block, Main tab → Tune). Simulink linearizes the engine model at the current operating point to get the plant the controller “sees,” and proposes an initial PI design. Q11 model-based tuning
  4. Initial design: settling ≈ 2 s, overshoot ≈ 7.5%. (“Show parameters.”) Settling already meets the 5 s spec. If this loop behaved like a standard second-order system, 7.5% OS would correspond to ζ ≈ 0.64; you're near the “balanced” point on the trade-off map. Q9 specs, ζ ↔ %OS
  5. Slide Response time to the LEFT (slower = longer response time). Result: 0% overshoot, settling ≈ 4 s, and the controller becomes effectively integral-only (P gain ≈ 0). Still meets < 5 s: you spent the spare settling-time budget to buy zero overshoot. Deep dive D: lower ωc ⇒ more PM
  6. Use both sliders: response time 3.4 → 1.5 s (faster) and robustness/transient behavior 0.6 → 0.72 (more robust). Result: 0% overshoot and settling < 2 s, better than the initial design on both counts. trade-off map: move right and up
  7. Update Block writes P and I into the Simulink block. Simulate the nonlinear model and confirm the step response still meets both requirements. linearization validity, saturation

What to record yourselfThe example doesn't list every gain value in its text, and gains differ slightly between MATLAB releases. For your report, copy P, I, rise time, settling time, overshoot, gain margin and phase margin from the “Show parameters” panel for each of the three designs, and screenshot each response. Also note: the slider's “response time” is a design target; it's not the same number as the measured settling time.
Q13

Why did each slider move do what it did?

Move 1: slower response ⇒ zero overshoot, ≈4 s, “integral-only”

Predict: slower means lower bandwidth, means less plant phase lag at crossover, so more phase margin, so less overshoot, at the cost of settling time. Observed: overshoot 7.5% → 0, settling 2 → 4 s. ✓

Why “integral-only”? Once the crossover is pushed low enough, an integrator by itself (gain falling at −20 dB/dec, phase −90°) already crosses 0 dB with plenty of margin, so the optimizer has no need for the P term. Remember the I-only second-order result (Q9): small Ki ⇒ large ζ ⇒ no overshoot, but slow. The I term alone still guarantees zero steady-state error, so the requirement holds.

Move 2: faster (3.4 → 1.5 s) + more robust (0.6 → 0.72) ⇒ zero overshoot, < 2 s

Predict each slider separately first:

  • Faster alone: higher ωc, so less PM, so overshoot comes back (likely above 7.5%).
  • More robust alone: more PM, so less overshoot, typically a bit slower.

Together: the speed slider sets ωc high (fast), and the robustness slider forces the tuner to place the PI zero/gain so the phase margin stays large at that higher ωc. The overshoot from the speed-up is cancelled by the added margin. That this works at all tells you the plant has enough phase “room” at that frequency; for a plant with more lag or delay, you'd hit the frontier and couldn't have both.

Why verify on the nonlinear model?

The tuner designed against a linearization at one operating point. The real engine model is nonlinear, so the gains may behave differently for larger steps or other speeds, and actuator limits (throttle saturation) are invisible to a linear design. Simulating the full model after Update Block confirms the design really meets < 5 s and zero steady-state error. If it doesn't, suspect operating-point mismatch or saturation/windup.

Q14

What are the common pitfalls?

PitfallWhy it's wrong / what happens
“More Kp eliminates steady-state error.”It only reduces it (1/(1+KpG(0))) on a type-0 plant. Zero error needs an integrator.
Mixing parallel and standard formsKi = Kp/Ti, Kd = KpTd. ZN tables are in standard form; the Simulink block defaults to parallel.
“Bigger Ti = more integral.”Opposite: bigger Ti = weaker I action.
Unfiltered derivativeNoise amplified ∝ ω. Always keep a filter (N) and use D only if you need it.

Changing several things at onceYou can't attribute the effect. One change per run, predicted in advance.

How should I explain my results in the report?

For each change: Predict (what and why, from theory) → Run → Observe (numbers) → Explain (mechanism, agree/disagree with prediction) → Check against requirements.
Template paragraph

Change: moved the response-time slider left (slower).
Prediction: a slower target lowers the loop bandwidth; at lower crossover frequency the plant adds less phase lag, so the phase margin increases and the overshoot should decrease, while the settling time should increase.
Observation: overshoot fell from ≈7.5% to 0%, settling time rose from ≈2 s to ≈4 s; the tuned controller became effectively integral-only (P ≈ 0).

Requirement check: Ts ≈ 4 s < 5 s ✓; ess = 0 ✓.

Cheat sheet

Forms

  • Parallel: Kp + Ki/s + Kds
  • Standard: Kp(1 + 1/(Tis) + Tds)
  • Ki = Kp/Ti · Kd = KpTd
  • Filtered D: KdNs/(s+N), HF gain KdN

Loop algebra

  • Y/R = CG/(1+CG) · Y/D = G/(1+CG)
  • E/R = 1/(1+CG)
  • Step ess = 1/(1 + C(0)G(0))
  • P: 1/(1+KpG(0)) · with I: 0

Gain effects (↑ gain)

  • Kp: faster, more OS, less (≠0) ess
  • Ki: ess → 0, more OS, slower settling
  • Kd: less OS, faster settling, noise ↑

2nd-order specs

  • %OS = 100 e−πζ/√(1−ζ²)
  • Ts(2%) ≈ 4/(ζωn) · Tp = π/ωd
  • ζ ≈ PM/100 (PM ≲ 65°, rule of thumb)
  • 7.5% OS ⇔ ζ ≈ 0.64

Ziegler–Nichols (ultimate)

  • P: 0.5Ku
  • PI: 0.45Ku, Ti = Tu/1.2
  • PID: 0.6Ku, Ti = Tu/2, Td = Tu/8
  • 1/(s+1)³: Ku = 8, Tu = 2π/√3 ≈ 3.63 s

PID Tuner & Project 1

  • Response time ← = slower, lower BW, less OS
  • Transient → Robust = more PM, less OS
  • Init: 2 s, 7.5% · Slower: 4 s, 0%, ≈I-only
  • 3.4→1.5 s & 0.6→0.72: 0%, < 2 s
  • Update Block → verify nonlinear

Glossary

Plant
The system being controlled (here: the engine), G(s).
Reference / set-point r
The desired output value.
Error e
e = r − y (measured). The PID controller's input.
Disturbance d
An unwanted input (load torque, hill) that pushes the output away from r.
Steady-state error ess
limt→∞ e(t). For a step: 1/(1 + C(0)G(0)) with unity feedback.
System type
Number of free integrators (poles at s = 0) in the loop gain CG. Type ≥ 1 ⇒ zero step error.
Overshoot (%OS)
How far the peak exceeds the final value, as a % of the final value.
Rise time
Time to go from 10% to 90% of the final value (MATLAB default).
Settling time
Time after which the response stays within ±2% of its final value (MATLAB stepinfo default).
Damping ratio ζ
Second-order parameter: 0 < ζ < 1 underdamped (overshoot), ζ = 1 critically damped, ζ > 1 overdamped.
Natural frequency ωn
Second-order “speed” parameter (rad/s); distance of the poles from the origin.
Loop gain L(s)
C(s)G(s)H(s), the product around the loop. Its frequency response determines stability margins.
Crossover frequency ωc
Frequency where |L(jω)| = 1. Sets the loop's bandwidth/speed.
Bandwidth
Frequency range over which the closed loop tracks well (≈ ωc). Higher ⇒ faster.
Phase margin (PM)
180° + ∠L(jωc). Distance from the −180° oscillation condition; larger ⇒ more robust, less overshoot.
Gain margin
Factor by which loop gain could increase before instability (at the −180° phase frequency).
Robustness
Ability to stay stable and perform well despite model error. In PID Tuner: the transient-behavior slider.
Response time (PID Tuner)
Design target for closed-loop speed; inversely related to bandwidth. Slider left = longer.
Ultimate gain Ku, period Tu
P-only gain giving sustained oscillation, and that oscillation's period (Ziegler–Nichols).
Derivative filter N
Limits D's high-frequency gain: KdNs/(s+N).
Derivative kick
Spike in u when D acts on e and r steps. Fix: D on measurement.
Saturation
Actuator hitting its physical limit.
Integrator windup
Integrator accumulating during saturation, causing large overshoot afterwards.
Anti-windup
Clamping or back-calculation to stop the integrator from winding up.
Linearization
Approximating a nonlinear model by a linear one around an operating point (what PID Tuner does before designing).
Operating point
The steady condition (e.g. a given engine speed/load) about which the model is linearized.

A1 · Slow responseIt gets there, but takes far too long to rise and settle.from Q3
Symptoms
  • Long rise time; smooth, no overshoot
  • Output creeps toward r and the error shrinks slowly
  • Control signal u stays small — the controller is barely pushing
What to try
  • Raise Kp first: more push when the error is big (faster rise).
  • Then raise Ki so the last part of the error vanishes faster (watch for overshoot).
  • Stop when overshoot or noise/effort starts to hurt: that is the speed limit (Q8). In PID Tuner this is dragging Response time to the right/shorter (Project 1 did exactly that: 3.4 s → 1.5 s).

Which knob? ↑ Kp (and Ki) — until overshoot or effort says stop.

↑ Back to the Q3 table

A2 · Steady-state offsetIt settles calmly, but not at the target.from Q3
Symptoms
  • Flat, steady line that stays below (or above) r
  • Error never decays to zero however long you wait
  • Often appears with P-only control or after a constant load appears
What to try
  • Add integral action (Ki > 0). I keeps growing while any error remains, so it only stops when e = 0 (Deep dive A).
  • Raising Kp alone only shrinks the offset (ess = 1/(1+Kp·G(0))), it never removes it.
  • Project 1 asks for zero steady-state error: that is why it is a PI controller.

Which knob? Add / increase Ki (I action). Kp only shrinks the offset.

↑ Back to the Q3 table

A3 · OvershootThe output shoots past the target, then comes back.from Q3
Symptoms
  • Peak above r (%OS), then a return, possibly with a small undershoot
  • Often caused by an integral term that is too strong for the plant speed
  • Lower phase margin ⇒ more overshoot (Deep dive D)
What to try
  • Lower Ki (most common cure): the integrator is overshooting because it has stored too much area.
  • Or lower Kp a bit, or add some Kd to brake the approach (costs noise sensitivity).
  • If the overshoot only appears on set-point steps, a set-point filter or weight (A13) removes it without slowing disturbance recovery.
  • In PID Tuner, moving Response time slower (left) reduced overshoot in the Project 1 example.

Which knob? ↓ Ki (first), or ↓ Kp / ↑ Kd.

↑ Back to the Q3 table

A4 · Sustained oscillation / instabilityThe loop rings forever or the amplitude grows.from Q3
Symptoms
  • Constant-amplitude ringing = loop sits right at the stability edge (P-only gain = Ku)
  • Growing swing, often hitting actuator limits = unstable
  • Period roughly the ultimate period Tu (Ziegler–Nichols)
What to try
  • Back off: cut Kp (and Ki). Lower gain at the frequency where phase lag reaches −180° is the fundamental cure.
  • A little Kd can add phase lead and calm it — but only if the sensor is clean.
  • If you must keep high gain, the plant has too much lag/delay for PID: look at A10 (delay) and A11 (sensor lag).
  • In PID Tuner this is the Robustness / transient behaviour slider (more robust = more phase margin).

Which knob? ↓ Kp (and Ki); more robustness / phase margin.

↑ Back to the Q3 table

A5 · Poor disturbance rejectionA load hits and the output is knocked far away, recovering slowly.from Q3
Symptoms
  • Big excursion of y right after the load arrives
  • Slow return (or a permanent offset if there is no I)
  • The set-point response may look fine: this is a separate requirement
What to try
  • Raise the loop gain at the disturbance's speed: ↑ Kp for a bigger, faster reaction, ↑ Ki so a constant load is eventually cancelled exactly.
  • If the disturbance can be measured (e.g. a load sensor), add feedforward: act before the output moves — PID alone can only react afterwards.
  • The limit is the same trade-off as always: the more aggressively you reject, the less phase margin you have (A4).

Which knob? ↑ Kp and Ki; feedforward if the disturbance is measurable.

↑ Back to the Q3 table

A6 · Noisy / jittery actuatorMeasurement noise is amplified into a chattering control signal.from Q3
Symptoms
  • y looks fine but u is fuzzy / chattering (the plant filters the noise, the actuator doesn't)
  • Heard or felt as buzzing valves, hot motors, wear
  • Worse with large Kd and large derivative-filter N
What to try
  • Filter the derivative: lower N (or the filter coefficient in the block) so D stops amplifying high frequencies (Deep dive B).
  • ↓ Kd, or drop D entirely — a PI controller (as in Project 1) has no D noise problem.
  • Filter or choose a better sensor; avoid raising Kp just to chase speed.

Which knob? ↓ N (filter D harder) or ↓ Kd.

↑ Back to the Q3 table

A7 · Integrator windup (actuator saturation)The integrator keeps charging while the actuator is pinned at its limit.from Q3
Symptoms
  • u flat at the limit for a while
  • Output overshoots far more than the linear design predicted, then recovers slowly
  • Gets worse for large steps or long saturation
What to try
  • Turn on anti-windup: clamping or back-calculation (Simulink PID block → Output Saturation tab).
  • Smaller steps, or ramp the set-point so the actuator never saturates.
  • Remember PID Tuner designs a linear loop; check the nonlinear model for this (Deep dive E).

Which knob? Not a gain: add anti-windup (clamp / back-calculation) or avoid saturating.

↑ Back to the Q3 table

A8 · Derivative kickA set-point step makes D produce a huge one-sample spike in u.
Symptoms
  • Sharp spike in u exactly when r jumps (≈ Kd·N on top of the normal P kick)
  • Output barely changes, but actuator slams (and may saturate)
  • No spike for slow set-point changes
What to try
  • Apply D to the measurement only (“derivative on measurement” / set-point weight 0 on D). It still damps the loop and ignores set-point jumps.
  • Or put a set-point filter / ramp in front of the loop (A13).
  • Lower N if the spike is still too big.

Which knob? Not a gain: D on measurement (or set-point filter).

↑ Back to the symptom table

A9 · Actuator saturation & rate limitsThe actuator can only deliver so much (amplitude) and so fast (rate).
Symptoms
  • Amplitude limit: response is slower than the linear design promised for big steps (rise set by umax, not by the gains)
  • Rate limit: extra lag ⇒ more overshoot / ringing than the linear model predicts
  • u is straight-line (ramp) or flat-topped instead of smooth
What to try
  • Don't demand what the actuator can't deliver: smaller steps or a set-point ramp (rate limiter on r).
  • With a rate limit, lower Kp / Ki (the loop is effectively slower than you thought).
  • Always pair limits with anti-windup (A7); if the limit is the real bottleneck, a bigger actuator beats any retuning.

Which knob? ↓ aggressiveness; ramp the set-point; anti-windup.

↑ Back to the symptom table

A10 · Time delay (dead time)Nothing happens for a while after u changes, so feedback acts on old news.
Symptoms
  • Flat start: the output ignores u for the delay
  • Gains that were fine without delay now ring or go unstable
What to try
  • Lower the gain a lot, especially Ki: integrating on delayed information winds the loop up.
  • D does not help (it can't see the future beyond the delay).
  • If the delay is large compared with the plant time constant, PID is the wrong tool: use a Smith predictor/model-based design, or reduce the delay physically (move the sensor).

Which knob? ↓↓ Kp and Ki; Smith predictor if delay dominates.

↑ Back to the symptom table

A11 · Measurement (sensor) lagThe sensor is slow, so the controller sees an old, smoothed copy of y.
Symptoms
  • True y overshoots and rings; the measured signal is a delayed, smoother copy
  • Behaves like extra phase lag/delay (compare A10) — gains too high for the real loop
  • Derivative acts on the lagged signal, so it helps less than expected
What to try
  • Detune: ↓ Kp and Ki until the true output is calm (the lag eats phase margin).
  • Best cure is hardware: faster sensor, less filtering, better placement.
  • Don't over-filter the measurement for noise (A6) unless you re-check stability.

Which knob? ↓ Kp and Ki; fix/replace the sensor if you can.

↑ Back to the symptom table

A12 · Nonlinearity / operating-point changeThe plant's gain depends on where it is operating, so one set of gains is right only near one point.
Symptoms
  • Same controller: fine for a small step, increasingly oscillatory for a bigger one (here incremental gain grows as 2√r)
  • Behaviour differs with load/speed/temperature
  • PID Tuner linearizes at one operating point — that is its blind spot (Project 1: verify on the nonlinear model)
What to try
  • Tune for the worst (highest-gain) operating point, accepting a slower response elsewhere.
  • Or use gain scheduling: change gains with the operating point (as the dashed curve does).
  • Always verify the linear design on the nonlinear simulation.

Which knob? ↓ gains for the high-gain point, or schedule them with operating point.

↑ Back to the symptom table

A13 · Set-point vs disturbance trade-off (2-DOF)Gains tuned to reject disturbances fast make the set-point step overshoot.
Symptoms
  • Strong integral action: quick recovery from the load, but big overshoot on the set-point step
  • Lowering gains fixes the step but slows the load recovery — a conflict with one controller
What to try
  • Use a two-degree-of-freedom structure: keep the feedback gains for disturbances, and shape the set-point separately (set-point weight b < 1 on P, or a set-point filter Tf).
  • The disturbance response is unchanged; only the set-point path is softened.
  • PID Tuner offers a 2-DOF controller type for this.

Which knob? Not a gain: set-point filter / weight (2-DOF).

↑ Back to the symptom table

A14 · Limit cycle from dead zone / backlashIntegral action plus a sticky actuator makes the loop hunt forever around the target.
Symptoms
  • Slow, constant-amplitude swing around r that never dies out
  • u creeps through the dead zone, nothing moves, then it jumps
  • The integrator keeps winding up inside the dead zone, then the actuator suddenly moves and overshoots
What to try
  • ↓ Ki (or turn I off) so the integrator can't keep winding up inside the dead zone; accept a small residual error.
  • Add a small controller dead band on the error, or repair the actuator (backlash/stiction).
  • This is a non-linear effect: linear tuning rules only help indirectly.

Which knob? ↓ Ki (or I off); fix the actuator.

↑ Back to the symptom table

How these fit togetherMost of these are the same trade-off in different clothes: more gain buys speed and disturbance rejection but costs phase margin, noise sensitivity and actuator effort. Delays, slow sensors, limits and nonlinearity shrink the room you have; they are why “just turn up the gain” eventually fails.
